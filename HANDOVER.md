## What the study found

The headline is a **negative, mechanistic result**, and it should be reported
as the finding rather than buried while hunting for a positive one:

- **Every prompting condition over-labels `TRIGGER` by three to six times.**
  The benchmark marks about 7% of calls as the vulnerable function; the
  conditions mark 23% to 41%. Recall is high, precision never exceeds about
  0.22. The model locates the flawed call but cannot separate it from the setup
  that enabled it or the drain it caused.
- **The conditions cannot be separated on F1.** Bootstrapping whole cases gives
  95% intervals that overlap heavily. With 28 cases a difference of 0.02 F1 is
  inside sampling noise. **Do not rank the conditions on F1 in either
  direction.**
- **Masking identifiers changes behaviour substantially**: about 41% of calls
  called `TRIGGER` with everything masked, about 26% with decoded names,
  measured over 1,335 calls. Report it as a behavioural effect, not as a
  significant accuracy gain.
- **No condition clearly beats a lexical rule baseline that costs nothing.**
  For a reader deciding whether to deploy this, that is the most useful
  sentence in the chapter.

## Three metric errors, which belong in the methodology

Each changed a conclusion. Reporting them is a strength, not an admission:

1. `hit@any` was reported alone, which flatters a model that labels 40% of
   calls `TRIGGER`. Precision and the trigger rate must always sit beside it.
2. Precision compared across transaction sizes looked like a collapse on large
   traces. The benchmark's own rate falls from 23.1% of calls in a ten-call
   transaction to 2.8% in a hundred-call one, so precision falls automatically.
   Lift over chance removes the effect; the claim was withdrawn.
3. Conditions ranked on point estimates gave two opposite answers as data
   arrived. Bootstrap intervals show neither ranking holds.

## What is in the repository

- **The tool**: `pipeline/analyse.py` takes any transaction hash, or several,
  or a file of them, replays it, verifies the replay against the on-chain
  receipt, and prints a role-labelled call tree. It is not limited to the 28
  benchmark cases. Describe it in Chapter 4 or 7 as the artefact the study
  produced, and repeat its caveat: the `TRIGGER` set is a shortlist to read,
  not a verdict.
- **Chapter 5 material**: `data/case_studies/` has one file per selected case,
  generated from the traces and predictions. Cases were chosen for what they
  expose, not for flattery: the largest trace, the deepest with a 42-times
  repeated exploit, a three-call exploit, and one whose replay could not be
  verified. The interpretation sections are deliberately left blank.
- **The corpus**: 28 transactions, 1,335 call frames, 25 of 28 replays verified
  against the on-chain receipt gas exactly. The three that are not are named
  and excluded, not dropped quietly. See `data/CHECKPOINT.md`.

## Things that must be described accurately

- **The annotation is LLM-drafted and author-reviewed**, not independent human
  annotation. Only rows marked `reviewed=yes` count as ground truth.
- **Cohen's kappa is unavailable** because there is one annotator. Report its
  absence as a limitation; do not substitute agreement between two model runs.
- **Long transactions are windowed** into at most 40 calls, forced by the
  provider's output-token limit. In a windowed case the model sees a slice, not
  the whole transaction.
- **The traces were fetched without an Etherscan key**, so contract names appear
  as bare addresses. Condition C2's contribution is decoded function names and
  arguments, not contract names.
- **One run per condition.** The provider allows 200,000 tokens per day and a
  condition-run costs 75,000 to 150,000, so repeat runs were not affordable.
  Run-to-run agreement is therefore not reported.
- **The victim contract's identity is withheld from the model until C5**,
  because it comes from the ground truth. C3 reveals only the attacker and the
  attack contract, both readable from the transaction itself.
